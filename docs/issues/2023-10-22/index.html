Another full week!

Excellent posts in pedagogy, sustainability, athletics, creativity, leadership, and more.

As the Israeli-Hamas war continues into its third week, schools and colleges are learning from both their initial responses to the crisis and from their efforts to create spaces for students to cope. This week, reflections on these topics surfaced across schools: in leadership, pedagogy, health and more. Echoes are also ringing from the polarization that has marked the recent culture wars in America. See the two feature articles for recent posts about whether schools should make political statements and, perhaps most important in my mind, reflections on how we can reduce polarizations by listening to each others’ stories.

Else in news: a surprising study about the health benefits of cycling in middle school, a pair of posts about cell phone bans, a helpful piece in pedagogy on how personalization can be embedded in whole class work, and another excellent reflection on Barnes & Nobles’ revival that reminds us of the value of decentralization and autonomy in decision making.

Last, see the A.I. section at the end for a case for A.I. not only as pedagogy, but as a topic across the curriculum.

These and more, enjoy!

Peter

Adam Gopnik On Heather Cox Richardson And Protecting Democracy

New Yorker · 09/25/23

“The United States, in this account, was a picture of a successful democracy until relatively recently. It was tested by the Civil War and the Second World War but survived both. Indeed, one crucial way in which democracies “scale up” is through warfare: no ideal of common citizenship is as pointed as comradeship in combat. But in recent decades, as the familiar story insists, citizenship and the ideal of negotiation and compromise have broken down through polarization intensified by social media… Demonizing “the other side” is a bad idea, but in a healthy democracy the real demons don’t get a side. Armed gangs and warlords, who have decided much of human history, don’t get a voice. Mussolini ceased to be a politician when he marched on Rome. We have to be prepared to have debates, and to lose, on questions that may at the moment seem to us matters of life and death—on abortion or mass incarceration or gun sanity, say. We are compelled to bargain with people who believe, however crazily, that guns promote social peace. But when they pull out guns the bargaining ends.”
