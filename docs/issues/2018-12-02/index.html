Introduction

Let's draw. Suddenly, several sources are exploring the value of drawing -- even bad drawing -- to support richly encoded learning. Some delightful news here to get us drawing with a pencil again.

Last week's article on college reading for incoming freshmen garnered significant interest, and I suspect many people would be interested in the report that offers much more detail on the books schools chose. That report can be found here .

A while back, I posted an article about "thick institutions" -- those that garner loyalty, identity, sense of common purpose. The Aeon video on dance explores how "collective effervescence" plays a role in building strong communities. Chalk this in the category of how to build culture.

Enjoy these and others!

“We have misfiled the significance of drawing because we see it as a professional skill instead of a personal capacity… This essential confusion has stunted our understanding of drawing and kept it from being seen as a tool for learning above all else… Drawing shouldn’t be about performance, but about process. Drawing Makes Richer Memories Than Writing

British Psychological Society · 11/22/18

“There are various components to the process of drawing a picture of a word or concept, each of which seem to cumulatively aid memory. Getting people to trace over an existing drawing (so getting them to make relevant arm and hand movements, but not allowing personal elaboration), or to create a drawing which they were then not allowed to see (so allowing the physical movements and personal elaboration, but depriving them of the visual memory of the end result) both improved memory – but not as much as when all of these stages were allowed.”

On “CUREs” Course-Based Undergraduate Research Experiences

Forbes · 11/26/18

“CUREs have five defining characteristics. First, they contain an element of discovery: the student scientists are bringing brand-new data to light. Second, the experimentation in which they engage involves “iteration”: repeating a process a number of times, altering a single variable to find out what happens. Third, participants experience a significant degree of collaboration, with their peers and with the expert scientists who are their instructors. Fourth, students learn how to execute professional practices, from using techniques like pipetting to applying meta-theories like the scientific method. And fifth, the subject of their investigations is “broadly relevant”—of interest to people outside the class and perhaps even worthy of publication in a scientific journal.”
