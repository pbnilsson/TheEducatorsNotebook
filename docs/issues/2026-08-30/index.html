Introduction

AI work at schools, science education in 2030, AI guidance, and more

An excellent week!

First, what a delight it has been to work with schools as the year has gotten started, especially those for whom Irreplaceable was their summer reading. Conversations have spanned from AI itself (how it works, how to use it effectively, how it has developed) to intersections with ethics, learning science, how different divisions might engage AI (teachers or students), and more. Don’t hesitate to reach out if your school is looking for support or guidance.

This week’s features cover two very different topics. First, former college president Paul LeBlanc pens an excellent exploration into the value of higher education in a time of digital transformation in many workplaces. He looks specifically at what parents and high school students might look for in a college or university as the world changes. Then also, in a time of continued political instability (chaos?), see a thoughtful piece on what voice educational institutions might have in public discourse.

Also this week: a few posts on curiosity and creativity, some reflections on the new (school) year, and a remarkable story of a Wall Street Journal op-ed written with the aid of AI — and the author and editor who defended it. See that last one in three parts in the Reading/Writing section.

Last: Claude for Teachers is now free for teachers and the enterprise version for schools is also free for schools. If you are looking for what may be highest quality enterprise foundation model — and also the one with what seems the most human-centered approach — this is a good opportunity. And it’s free, at least for now. See the AI Update for more information.

These and more, enjoy!

Peter

No letting AI draft a column or editorial. Our readers need to know we’re not subcontracting our ideas or prose to the amalgamation of a machine. A.I. Update

Claude for Teachers, surveys, AI detectors, and more

Each week seems to outdo the previous…

In this issue’s features, check out the survey by Common Sense Media on how students feel about school and AI. What I particularly appreciate is that it captures sentiments beyond AI itself, which helps provide some context to the sentiments about AI. Also in the features, the essay by Michael Wagner in his Augmented Educator blog nicely focuses in on the single factor that matters most about the risks of using AI detectors. It’s a worthy read.

Also, I had previously highlighted Claude for Teachers as a great, free option for teachers. Now Anthropic has made an enterprise version available to K-12 schools. This is an excellent option for schools that want both to have (what I think is) the highest quality tool and also to use the tool that seems to have the best moral/ethical compass of all the AI companies. LLM wrappers like FlintK-12 and others make good specific tools for schools; this is the best foundation model option.

In the bigger picture, the Ethics and Risk section catalogs the growing concern about the safety issues emerging today. Bill Gates spells out three specific concerns in a lengthy essay.

And most practically, see the Uses and Applications section for some helpful prompts to pursue new directions of thinking and inquiry with AI tools.

Don’t Use AI Detectors: Too Much Is Still Left To Inconsistent Judgment

Augmented Educator · 08/20/26

“A false-positive rate of 0.19 percent means that out of 1,000 human-written essays, the detector falsely flags only about two texts as AI-generated. Sounds great, doesn’t it? But unfortunately, none of those numbers can really tell you what will happen in your classroom. Because outside of a controlled lab environment, they have very little meaning… A school, or any educational institution for that matter, has to choose which of the two errors it is more willing to accept. But the problem is that these errors do not carry equal consequences. Missing an AI-written essay compromises an assessment. This is most likely not a big deal. Falsely accusing a student, however, can derail a degree and follow that student for years.”

Report: How Do Kids Use And Feel About AI In School In 2026?

Common Sense Media · 08/18/26

“Few teens are being taught AI literacy. Only 27% of teens say a teacher has ever talked with them about how to use AI safely; 73% say they have not. Bans don't seem to be closing AI literacy gaps. Among teens who use AI for schoolwork, 44% have had an AI tool blocked on a school network or device, and 59% of them just switched to a personal device to get around the ban. Teens see the cost themselves. Among those who use AI, 38% say having it leads them to generate fewer of their own ideas, and 39% say they feel like they're missing out on learning when they use AI to complete assignments.”
