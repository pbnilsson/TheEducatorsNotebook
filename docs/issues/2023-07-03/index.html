Happy summer and holiday weekend, readers!

It's been an excellent break since the last issue, but I'm returning to the routine. New topics are urgently arising in preparation for this fall, and the news is awash with critical happenings:

• Advances in A.I. continue to complicate learning.

• Advances in A.I. also continue to supercharge learning.

• Supreme Court rulings are challenging diversity efforts.

• Admissions offices are innovating in response.

• The post-pandemic need for good governance grows.

• Social Media continues to cause concern.

• And on the positive and most essential, teaching & learning research continues to yield evidence of what makes for excellent practice.

This week's issue (which catches up on a few previous weeks) captures articles across these topics and much, much more.

On A.I., see the featured blog post by researcher Ethan Mollick on some of the key ways homework will need to evolve in the year ahead. Also see Nate Green's piece in Slate on the need for schools to act now (as in this summer) on how we will proactively set students and teachers up for success in a changed world with A.I.. Green highlights schools' failures (and culture's broader failure) to proactively engage the arrival of social media, and the cost of this is apparent in this week's social media articles, which show the challenges we are still facing today.

Clear policies and practices will help schools engage the emergence of A.I. both responsibly and in ways that empower student learning and teacher growth. Keep an eye on upcoming issues for more on this, and see the featured Learning Activities for the beginnings of a list that I will be accumulating of different practices for students and teachers alike.

Also see the other feature on how admissions offices might pivot in response to the Supreme Court ruling on affirmative action. Affirmative action helped countless students. See also how one student calls for something stronger. The best and boldest find ways to make a better future from even the most difficult losses.

Find all these and much, much more in this week's issue -- including the story of a 14 year old who made his own LEGO animation of Spiderman... and was called on by the producers to animate a scene in the actual film.

Enjoy!

Peter

“The Homework Apocalypse:” The Essay, Reading, And The Problem Set

One Useful Thing · 07/01/23

“Students will cheat with AI. But they also will begin to integrate AI into everything they do, raising new questions for educators. Students will want to understand why they are doing assignments that seem obsolete thanks to AI. They will want to use AI as a learning companion, a co-author, or a teammate. They will want to accomplish more than they did before, and also want answers about what AI means for their future learning paths. Schools will need to decide how to respond to this flood of questions. The challenge of AI in education can feel abstract, so to understand a bit more about what is going to happen, I wanted to examine some common assignment types… There is light at the end of the AI tunnel for educators, but it will require experiments and adjustment. In the meantime, we need to be realistic about how many things are about to change in the near future, and start to plan now for what we will do in response to the Homework Apocalypse. Fall is coming.”

New York Times · 06/20/23

“The paper tried to address one of the most persistent challenges for brain trauma researchers: identifying what aspects of head hits contribute most to C.T.E. They looked at the number of hits to the head, the number of years playing football, the force of those hits and other factors. Jill Lepore, Constitutional Amendments, And The Need For Ongoing Change

New York Times · 07/01/23

“A written constitution ratified by the people — and subject to amendment by the people — is an American invention. In the 18th century, people who drafted constitutions and commented on constitutionalism came to agree that if such a strange, new and fragile thing as a written constitution were to endure, it would, as time passed, need to be both repaired and improved, mended and amended. To amend meant, at the time, to correct a fault; to repair an omission; to fix what’s broken; or to improve, in a moral sense: to make something better. The word shares a root, four of its five letters, and almost the entirety of its meaning, with the verb “mend.””

Some Basic Research: How Teacher Use Of Class Time Affects Student Performance

Science Direct · 04/17/23

“Students score higher in math when their teacher devotes more class time to individual practice and assessment. In contrast, students score higher in English if there is more discussion and work with classmates. Class time allocation predicts test scores separate from the quality of the teacher's instruction during the activities. These results suggest opportunities to improve student achievement without changes in teachers’ skills.”
