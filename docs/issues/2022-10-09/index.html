Introduction

Two good weeks these past two weeks.

I was out with COIVD and am back now (it's coming for you if it hasn't reach you yet...) with some good reading for the week ahead. Writing across the web continues to focus on the downstream effects of the pandemic and how we can support educators in this extraordinary time.

The first feature article reminds us that support is essential when setting high standards, and this is echoed in Pasi Sahlberg's redefinition of equity. I'm reminded of Richard Elmore's principle of reciprocity (2000): that for every unit of accountability we expect as teachers and leaders we have an obligation to provide an equivalent unit of support or capacity.

The second feature reminds us that doing few things better is what enables us to focus and excel. We see many iterations of this principle: more academic schedules are moving to fewer classes per day with longer blocks, Steve Jobs notably said that focus isn't saying yes to something, it's saying no to a thousand other things. Mike Schmoker's book Focus emphatically made this case for schools in 2014.

Also this week: a drama performed through text message, MIT profs propose an evolved model of higher ed, Linda Darling-Hammond proposes a new model for K12 schools, an excellent piece of humor (and seriousness) by the Onion, an interview of Atul Gawande by Adam Grant, and more.

These and more, enjoy!

FEATURED

“The Power Of Doing Less In Schools”

ASCD · 10/01/22

“If the system has to be fixed, and we can't fix the system by adding to it, then the logical place to start is with subtraction. We need to look closely at our schools and figure out everything that we don't need to be doing anymore. We need to find as many things as possible that we can take off the plates of overworked educators. At its heart, the art of subtraction is clearing away peripheral parts of a system so that we can put better focus on the most important things.”

Rigor Without Support Misses The Needs Of Today’s Students

New York Times · 10/07/22

“Courses that are meant to distinguish between serious and unserious students, it has become clear, often do a better job distinguishing between students who have ample resources and those who don’t… Instead, universities should focus on the broader goal of teaching for equity and with empathy, which means ensuring that students get the support they need to learn and succeed, without petitions and even without having to ask.”
