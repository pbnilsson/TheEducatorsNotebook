Introduction

An extraordinary week.

Two excellent feature posts: one in which the NYT deep dives on how girls navigate social media today (with a follow up in the section on Adolescence), and another on a helpful frame for what care for kids looks like.

In addition to a host of compelling and provocative pieces this week, see the Workplace section for some interesting commentary on the changes of dress code requirements in the US Senate. I found one opinion piece particularly interesting for its observation that loosening dress codes signifies a drifting away from consensus. This reflects a much larger arc in a shift from the community to the individual. I will be thinking about this for a while. In what ways does/can a dress code show a consensus, a collective agreement about some element of identity that we all share? What might this look like?

Finally, it was a deluge in the tech/AI space this week. The coming weeks will continue to see a surge as students, teachers, and parents begin to reckon with the changing world around us. If you're feeling behind the ball and need to catch up, see the overview by Cornell University. It is comprehensive in context, policy, and practical usage recommendations.

These and others, enjoy!

Peter

“What We Love When We Loosen Dress Codes”

New York Times · 09/22/23

“Dress codes are a marker of social, national, professional or philosophical commonality. They bespeak shared ideals or training, membership in a group… the Senate is more than just a “workplace.” It represents the highest level of our country’s government, whose actions are watched by and hold consequences for the entire world… Such an august body needs to look the part. A sea of 100 adults all dressed in some kind of instantly recognizable, respectful manner — a suit and tie, a skirt and jacket — creates a unified visual entity. A group in which individuals have agreed to subsume their differences into an overarching, sartorial whole. But as we all know, the Senate has never been more divided. In a body so riven, one of the last symbolic markers of accord is a dress code. Can such a code eliminate the profound differences beneath the surface? Of course not. But it does remind senators and everyone around them (including the general public) of the still-noble goal of consensus.”
