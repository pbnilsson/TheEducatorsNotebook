Introduction

Conversation science, finding a middle ground on AI, the match of M.C. Escher, and more

It’s been a busy October and early November — with the usual, inexorable change around us. In this issue’s features find four (4! — it’s been a minute since the last issue) posts that show just how much our AI moment is pushing learning in two directions at the same time: both into and away from the technology. Two posts focus on analog actions — humanities classes and long form reading — and two others focus on seismic shifts in today’s world: the prospect of giving up on homework and writing in an entirely new way. Much food for thought here.

On these themes, find posts this week on conversation science, fitness mindsets, prescribing art to feed your brain, the math behind M.C. Escher’s art, and much more.

These and more, enjoy!

A.I. Update

Four currents of social change from AI, parent perspectives, and more

Well, six weeks of AI news is a lifetime of news. This issue holds some of the highlights from the last month and a half.

In the five (5!) features, find more on the recent safety questions, some recent technical developments, and, most importantly (I believe), the reflection by the Rithm Project on the subtle ways AI is changing our social interactions. You’ve heard me say that this will be the most significant impact AI has on us. Not the job loss or the learning loss, but the changes to how we relate to each other socially and emotionally. The Rithm Project has identified four early subtle shifts: it blurring our sense of what is authentic and trustworthy; we are moving from building relationships through technology to building relationships with technology itself; AI is shaping our inner voice, changing how we understand and relate to ourselves; and AI is acting on our behalf, absorbing work that relationships are built on. This small nudges have large impacts at scale and over time. See the report for more.

Also in this issue, a survey of parents about what they hope schools will do about/with AI. The strength of this report is how much overlap it surfaces. See the image at the top of this issue.

Also, see responses from mathematicians to the extraordinary work AI had proposed in the math field.

These and more, enjoy!

What Is The Key To Expanding AI Intelligence? The “Bitter Lesson”

New York Times · 10/30/26

“On the very first page, Anthropic refers to a concept known as the “bitter lesson” — that when it comes to A.I. innovation, brute-force power will consistently replace handcrafted solutions… Famously, IBM’s chess-playing computer, Deep Blue, which used custom chips and supercomputer speed, beat the reigning world chess champion, Garry Kasparov, in 1997 by searching through 200 million chess positions per second. By 2017, Google DeepMind’s AlphaZero project had become even more generalized: Google fed the rules of chess to AlphaZero software, it played millions of games against itself, and it became extremely good at chess in about nine hours. This pattern — brute force replacing handcrafted solutions — proved itself out, decade after decade. That is the bitter lesson, a term coined in 2019 by the computer scientist Richard Sutton.”
