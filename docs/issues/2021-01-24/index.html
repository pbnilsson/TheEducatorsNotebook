Introduction

An excellent week full of news and opportunity:

The U.S. presidential inauguration sparked a broad range of writing for educators this week, and especially Amanda Gorman's poem "The Hill We Climb." Beyond the poem itself, I was caught by a detail that surfaced in an interview: that before every reading, Gorman recites a mantra of sorts: "I am the daughter of Black writers.," she says to herself. "We are descended from freedom fighters who broke their chains and changed the world. They call me."

This kind of practice is at the heart of some very compelling equity-focused research. In a 2011 landmark literature review (the first feature this week), Yeager and Walton note that small social-psychological interventions can erase the achievement gap, including one particular intervention that involves revisiting one's core values and beliefs before assessment performances. When we know who we are and what matters to us, we better overcome the pressures and threats that we face.

Other articles this week include: on the definition of being a generalist, on the benefit of admitting our mistakes, and on the increasingly complex world of affording a college education -- especially with the emergence of "merit aid" (as different from financial aid, more generally).

Lastly, I'm a believer that sitting down and having an earnestly inquisitive conversation with others bridges most gaps and fosters understanding and connection, especially across difference. The card game noted in the diversity and inclusion section looks like it isn't designed as a tool for inclusion work formally, but I imagine it's design promotes the same principles.

Enjoy these and other pieces this week!

Social-Psychological Interventions In Education: They’re Not Magic

CSUN · 01/06/11

“Recent randomized experiments have found that seemingly “small” socialpsychological interventions in education—that is, brief exercises that target students’ thoughts, feelings, and beliefs in and about school—can lead to large gains in student achievement and sharply reduce achievement gaps even months and years later. These interventions do not teach students academic content but instead target students’ psychology, such as their beliefs that they have the potential to improve their intelligence or that they belong and are valued in school. “
