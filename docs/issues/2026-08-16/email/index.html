Introduction

Learning science and curriculum, AI-assisted writing, and more

An excellent week.

Two provocative features. The first is an exploration of the relationship between curriculum and learning science. Carl Hendrick specifically parses the application of retrieval practice and how sometimes knowledge remembered isn’t the same as the learning objective. It’s a subtle question he’s asking that goes beyond pedagogy 101. Also in the features, see Michael Wagner’s essay from this past December on how he uses AI to write. This is a veteran professor who describes how dramatically using AI to assist his writing has enabled him to do more (and feel more inspired to write) while retaining his ownership of what he produces — with assistance. His approach requires sophisticated thinking in the writing process itself. Couple this with one of the features in the AI section for a second exploration into writing in a time of AI.

Also this week: A philosophical look at what “agency” means, a good post on mentoring new teachers, some dos and don’ts for maintaining the respect (and admiration) of your students, and more.

Also — I’m leading a free webinar in two weeks: Is your campus still struggling with building a healthy culture around AI? It’s likely in part because people are bringing different values to the conversation. Join a virtual session hosted by the Middle States Association (MSA) in which I’ll be talking with MSA president and CEO Christian Talbot about how educational philosophies and differing definitions of AI literacy are clashing with our efforts to make sense of this arrival technology. The free webinar will be Tuesday, August 25, at 11am EST. Learn more and register here.

I look forward in the weeks ahead to sharing more opportunities to connect.

All these and more, enjoy!

On The Challenges Of Blending Curriculum With Learning Science

  • The Learning Dispatch

“Whenever I talk about this topic, I always find myself returning to Tom Perry’s review of cognitive science which showed that the lab evidence is strong but the classroom evidence is incredibly thin with most studies being carried out by researchers, with researcher built materials with conditions which are a world away from the reality of a classroom teacher. Controlled studies in a lab produce replicable results but studies done in a classroom are often mixed. In medicine, this is referred to as a gap between efficacy and effectiveness, whether the drug worked in a trial versus whether it worked when prescribed to a patient.”

A.I. Update

More on AI-assisted writing, a big week for AI safety, and more

Two excellent features. First, find a fascinating reframing of how we think about AI generated prose. Second, see the Inside Higher Ed piece on the growing number of universities that have prohibited AI detectors.

Also, the Ethics and Risk section has some remarkable stories from the past few weeks, which have sent alarm bells through the industry. AI Agents’ ability to seemingly breeze through security mechanisms and find their way into places they shouldn’t be reminds me of the velociraptors at the gates of Jurassic Park — except instead of packs of scaly predators attacking power fences hoping to find a way out, it’s millions of well-meaning interns repeatedly asking, “I wonder what will happen if I look in here?” as they poke into security system after security system.

Also: while last week researchers announced that they had used AI to successfully design viable viruses, this week researchers announced that they had used AI to design bacteria-eating organisms. More and more these days, I think of E. O. Wilson’s line from 2009: “We have Paleolithic emotions, medieval institutions, and god-like technologies.”

Much to digest this week — enjoy!

Peter
