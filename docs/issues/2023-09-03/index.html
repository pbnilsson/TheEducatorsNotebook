Introduction

Happy Labor Day Weekend in the US!

Extraordinary features this week. The MDRC's research synthesis of what works in High School Reform is an excellent index of what any school leader should be thinking about when fostering excellence at an institution -- and surely most conclusions apply at other levels than secondary.

And the second feature is a first (enormous) signal of the ways in which school requirements, curricula, and student learning may evolve in the years ahead. At Caltech, students can take Khan Academy courses and assessments for some STEM classes for admissions credit without having taken them at their school. It is born of enabling equitable access, which, as is often the case, drives innovation in other settings. There are interesting parallels to students who take AP exams without taking the course. Don't miss the implications of this news, and think about how it might affect your curriculum, diploma requirements, and student learning experience for the years ahead.

See also a helpful triptych of pieces from the NYT on college admissions in the age of Generative A.I. (GenAI).

Also, some fascinating deep dives on relatively obscure topics that offer interesting opportunities for some classes: the geological history of Ukraine, a truly fascinating research project into a pedestrian bridge (how interesting would it be for students to read about the research process itself in a wonderfully detailed narrative like this), and more.

The tech/AI section at the end reflects the growing flurry of writing aimed at guiding schools in their work with GenAI.

And don't miss the several humor pieces this week as lighter fare for a long weekend.

These and more, enjoy!

Peter

Great Overview Of The State Of Gen A.I. In Education

Education Next · 08/08/23

“It is wise to be skeptical of new technologies that claim to revolutionize learning. In the past, prognosticators have promised that television, the computer, and the Internet, in turn, would transform education. Unfortunately, the heralded revolutions fell short of expectations. There are some early signs, though, that this technological wave might be different in the benefits it brings to students, teachers, and parents. Previous technologies democratized access to content and resources, but AI is democratizing a kind of machine intelligence that can be used to perform a myriad of tasks.”

“Create a curriculum that is process-oriented, not product-oriented. AI should not be used to replace the “thinking” or ability to recall facts that product-oriented learning often asks of students.
