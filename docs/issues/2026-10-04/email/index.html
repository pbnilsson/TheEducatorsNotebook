Introduction

Grading, youth today, and the role of writing in thinking

As ever, it’s been a remarkable few weeks in education. I’ve missed the past few issues, and I will be missing a few more during the month of October while on the road for a series of projects. So this issue is necessarily short.

Nonetheless, some thought provoking posts! In the features, find insights on today’s youth from two perspectives. From the macro perspective, find the results of a helpful survey on how kids are doing these days generally. The survey is another great reminder of the importance of relationships for learning. Then from the personal perspective, Nikole Hannah-Jones offers a moving reflection on her choice of schools for her daughter, who is now a teenager.

Elsewhere in the issue, a university walks back its move away from grading. Also find ideas for improving schools, a different look at attention, and more.

Also, it’s only one month to the mid-term elections. Here’s the cast of Hamilton with a Get Out the Vote remix of songs from Hamilton (including one from the King…).

These and more, enjoy!

A.I. Update

Existential risk rises, engage your students, how AI is changing medicine, and more

Well, the story of the month/summer/year seems to be rogue AI. Find quite a few posts on the topic in the Ethics and Risk section.

But also, the features host two excellent posts. First, Eric Hudson offers characteristically excellent counsel on engaging students around AI. And second, find a reflection from a physician on how AI is changing the medical profession. It offers meaningful insights for educators.

Then, elsewhere in the issue, see the Uses and Applications section for a post from Ernst & Young articulating the risk of losing your individuality and unique voice if you use AI. They approach this from a business angle that remarkably aligns with concerns many English and Humanities teachers are raising. It all makes sense, of course, given the statistical nature of how LLMs work — all the more reason for sound functional literacy of how AI works.

“Models Don’t Go Rogue”

  • Cybernetic Forests

“If you optimize a model to find exploits, you should expect it to find them — and prepare for that. OpenAI did not. They built a model, took the safeguards off, gave it the ExploitGym task, let it run, and didn't even monitor it. That's human decision-making. When it evaporates, what's left is the system from nowhere: a boundary focused on the technical system, rather than the decisions that build it. A stochastic flock machine can do lots of troubling things, particularly when we disavow our responsibility for shaping its direction, monitoring its output, or abandoning our capacity to intervene. These are tensions at the heart of agentic system design. But the "rogue" frame adds to this list of worries, offering up fantasies of a machine getting smarter. My worry is the intelligence that is retreating: the human intelligence that builds these systems, deploys them, and adopts them into workflows, then hides behind the results – then pins the blame on a system from nowhere.”
